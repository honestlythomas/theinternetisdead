# The Last Opponent

## Goku Surrenders the Chase and Returns to Grandpa Gohan

*A Dragon Ball narrative concept by Thomas Harrison, written for MasakoX*

MasakoX,

This idea begins with a simple question: what happens when Goku's appetite for challenge finally exceeds the universe's ability to provide him with opponents?

Goku is an unstoppable object. His entire life has been forward motion: the next master, the next rival, the next world, the next transformation, the next self to surpass. Food can interrupt him because hunger returns him to his body. Fighting gives him direction because an opponent returns his energy to him as resistance. But eventually there must be no meaningful “next.”

At that point, Goku asks for the one thing he has never faced: an opponent he cannot beat.

He expects a warrior.

What he finds is Grandpa Gohan's grave—the grave he never made as a child.

This is the story of Goku learning that the best way to win the final fight is not to begin it.

---

## I. The Man Who Cannot Stop

The story opens on Beerus's world.

Goku and Vegeta are sparring without transformations. Whis has forbidden them from using ki blasts, divine techniques, or even flight. The exercise is supposed to teach them economy of motion.

Goku attacks constantly.

Vegeta barely moves.

Every time Goku advances, Vegeta takes half a step aside and lets him pass. Goku accelerates. Vegeta refuses to match him. The angrier Goku becomes, the less Vegeta does.

Finally, Goku stops and demands to know why Vegeta will not fight properly.

Vegeta answers, “Because you are already fighting enough for both of us.”

Goku laughs, assumes this is another insult, and attacks again. Vegeta sits down.

Goku's fist stops inches from his face.

“What are you doing?” Goku asks.

“Choosing.”

Goku looks to Whis, expecting him to correct Vegeta. Instead, Whis smiles.

“An action you cannot refuse is not instinct, Goku. It is compulsion.”

Goku does not understand the distinction. His body is responsive. His movements are faster and cleaner than ever. He can enter Ultra Instinct without the catastrophic failure that once tore through him during the Tournament of Power. But he still cannot remain there. The state disappears whenever he becomes excited by his own improvement.

He keeps trying to catch it.

The harder he chases Ultra Instinct, the further away it moves.

Goku asks Whis for another exercise. Whis tells him there are no exercises left.

Goku asks for another opponent. Whis tells him every opponent available will only deepen the same habit.

Goku grins.

“Then find me one I can't beat.”

Whis studies him for a long moment.

“Do you want an opponent who is stronger than you,” he asks, “or one who cannot be defeated?”

Goku sees no difference.

Whis does.

---

## II. The Wish Without Dragon Balls

Goku searches anyway.

He asks Beerus, who refuses because Goku's request sounds exhausting. He asks Broly, but Broly is learning that training does not require losing control. He asks Jiren, who has already discovered that strength without relationship becomes a prison. He asks Hit, who says there is no contract. He even visits Arale, who knocks him through a mountain, becomes distracted by something ridiculous, and leaves before Goku can call the encounter a fight.

Everyone has somewhere else to be.

For the first time, Goku experiences the universe not as an infinite supply of resistance, but as a collection of people whose lives do not exist to complete his training.

He visits the Sacred World of the Kai.

Supreme Kai assumes Goku wants access to some divine metal, forgotten warrior, sealed demon, or forbidden realm. Goku explains that he needs something permanent: an opponent that will not tire, surrender, die, become his friend, or eventually be surpassed.

Elder Kai asks why.

Goku says, “Because if I can fight something like that, I'll finally know how far I can go.”

Elder Kai laughs—not because the request is funny, but because Goku has accidentally confessed the entire problem.

“You still think 'far' is where the answer lives.”

Supreme Kai places two fingers against Goku's forehead.

Goku expects a distant planet.

They teleport to Mount Paozu.

No enemy is waiting.

There is only the old forest, the little house, the sound of insects, and a silence Goku has spent most of his life outrunning.

Supreme Kai leaves without explaining.

---

## III. The Missing Grave

Goku searches the mountain.

At first he treats the task like a hunt. He closes his eyes and reaches for an unfamiliar ki. He listens for controlled breathing, a hidden footstep, the displacement of leaves. He circles the house. He checks the trees. He calls into the forest.

Nothing answers.

Then he notices the empty place near the house.

There is no grave for Grandpa Gohan.

Goku remembers fragments rather than a complete event: the full moon, waking alone, the absence he could not explain, and the four-star Dragon Ball sitting inside the house like a small orange sun. As a child, he did not understand burial. He did not possess the language or ritual for grief. He simply decided the ball contained Grandpa's presence.

Then Bulma arrived.

The ball became a reason to leave. The journey began. Every horizon offered another person, danger, meal, lesson, and fight. Goku never had to remain beside the original absence long enough to understand it.

He has crossed Snake Way. He has trained under gods. He has died, returned, watched friends die, gathered Dragon Balls, reversed catastrophes, crossed timelines, and fought in a tournament where entire universes vanished.

But he never buried the first person he lost.

Goku sits in the empty patch of ground.

Within minutes he becomes restless.

He stands. He stretches. He considers training while he waits for the mysterious opponent to arrive. He throws one punch, then another. The mountain returns no applause and offers no resistance. He begins a familiar sequence, faster each time, until his feet cut lines through the dirt.

Then he sees them.

Small footprints beneath his own.

They are only impressions from memory, but suddenly Goku can see the child who lived here: wild, hungry, powerful, and completely incapable of understanding that the person who loved him was gone.

Adult Goku stops moving.

The mysterious opponent is not late.

This is the place.

---

## IV. The First Thing Goku Builds Slowly

Goku could make a grave in seconds.

He could blast a perfect opening into the earth. He could lift a mountain and carve it with a finger. He could ask Bulma for machinery, Piccolo for help, or Shenron to create a monument visible from space.

Instead, he finds an old shovel.

The handle is too small for him. The metal is rusted. It may have belonged to Grandpa Gohan.

Goku begins to dig.

He digs without using ki.

The work is awkward. The ground is dense with roots and stones. Sweat forms on his forehead, not because the task exceeds his power, but because he is choosing to meet the earth at its own speed. Every time instinct tells him to finish faster, he loosens his grip.

Hours pass.

For once, endurance does not mean forcing himself beyond a limit. It means remaining present without manufacturing one.

He finds no body. Time and wilderness have already completed what the child could not. The grave will contain no remains. It is not a container for Grandpa Gohan.

It is an acknowledgement that Grandpa Gohan was here—and that he ended.

Goku chooses a stone and carries it back without flying. He cannot remember every lesson Grandpa taught him, but he remembers the feeling of being fed. He remembers a hand correcting his stance. He remembers being told not to look at the full moon. He remembers safety before he knew the word.

He carves four small stars into the marker.

Not because the Dragon Ball was Grandpa Gohan, but because believing it was allowed a lonely child to keep moving.

When the grave is finished, the sun is setting.

Goku sits beside it.

He does not know what to say.

So, for the first time in his life, he lets not knowing remain unanswered.

---

## V. Grandpa Gohan Refuses the Rematch

“Your shoulders are too high.”

Goku turns.

Grandpa Gohan is standing behind him exactly as Goku remembers: maskless, smiling, carrying a small bag of food.

Goku launches himself forward and embraces him. For a moment he is a child again. Then his adult habits return. He wipes his face, steps backward, and grins.

“Did Baba bring you? How long do we have? We should fight before you go. I've gotten way stronger.”

Grandpa Gohan sits beside the grave.

“No.”

Goku assumes he misheard.

Grandpa Gohan opens the bag and begins arranging a meal.

“You don't want to see how strong I am?”

“I can see you.”

“But you haven't seen what I can do now.”

“That isn't what I said.”

Goku stands frozen in a fighting stance that no longer has a partner.

Grandpa Gohan gestures to the ground beside him.

Goku sits.

They eat.

At first Goku fills the silence with stories. He tells Grandpa Gohan about Master Roshi, Krillin, Piccolo, Vegeta, Gohan, Goten, Pan, Beerus, Whis, other universes, and transformations that change the colour of his hair. He explains enemies who became friends and friends who became stronger than enemies. He talks faster as the light disappears.

Grandpa Gohan listens.

Eventually Goku runs out of chronology.

“Are you proud of me?” he asks.

Grandpa Gohan looks surprised.

It is the first opponent Goku has ever placed in front of that question.

“I was proud of you when you brought home enough firewood without knocking down the house.”

Goku laughs, but Grandpa Gohan does not let the joke carry them away.

“I was proud of you before the world knew your name. Before you saved anyone. Before you won anything. You were already Goku.”

Goku looks toward the grave.

“I killed you.”

“You were a child who did not understand his own body.”

“I should have controlled it.”

“You think responsibility means finding a way to make the child as powerful as the man.” Grandpa Gohan shakes his head. “Sometimes responsibility belongs to the adult who should have protected the child from a moon he did not understand.”

Goku has no technique for receiving forgiveness that does not require repayment.

“Then what am I supposed to do?”

Grandpa Gohan smiles.

“You are doing it.”

They remain beside the grave.

---

## VI. The Opponent Who Never Existed

Goku finally tells Grandpa Gohan why he returned.

“I asked for someone I couldn't beat.”

“And did you find him?”

Goku looks at the grave marker.

“I don't know.”

“Good.”

Goku frowns. “Whis keeps saying my body should move on its own. Sometimes it does. But when I notice it happening, I lose it. So I train harder, and then it gets further away. I thought if I found the right opponent—”

“You would become the right Goku?”

The sentence lands harder than any strike.

Grandpa Gohan explains that Goku has mistaken constant response for freedom. Every powerful opponent chooses Goku's next action simply by appearing stronger. Every new limit commands him to cross it. Every transformation creates another identity he must maintain or surpass.

Goku has become the strongest free man in the universe while remaining unable to refuse an invitation to fight.

“But fighting is who I am,” Goku says.

“Fighting is something you love.”

“What's the difference?”

“If you stop doing something you love, you miss it. If you stop doing what you think you are, you disappear.”

Goku looks frightened—not of Grandpa Gohan, death, or defeat, but of the empty space after motion.

“What happens if I stop?”

Grandpa Gohan answers, “You catch up to yourself.”

There is the final opponent.

Not Grandpa Gohan. Not the grave. Not death. Not an unbreakable wall.

The opponent is Goku's belief that he must forever become someone else in order to remain himself.

He has spent his life surpassing his reflection before it can settle into a recognizable face.

Ultra Instinct was never the destination. It was the choice to recognize the self as the origin point. Beginner's mind. Action without the burden of proving who acts.

Grandpa Gohan does not tell him to surrender fighting. He tells him to surrender running.

“You can fight tomorrow,” he says. “Tonight, you can stay.”

For Goku, staying is harder.

So he stays.

---

## VII. One Day Without Reversal

At sunrise, Baba appears at the edge of the clearing.

Goku immediately begins bargaining. There must be more time. The Dragon Balls can restore Grandpa Gohan permanently. Supreme Kai can make an exception. Whis can reverse time. Goku knows gods who owe him favours.

Grandpa Gohan places a hand on his shoulder.

“No.”

It is the same refusal as the rematch.

The old Goku would interpret death as a separation demanding action. The new choice is almost unbearably small: allow Grandpa Gohan to leave because Grandpa Gohan has chosen to leave.

“Will I see you again?”

“You stopped long enough to see me now.”

Grandpa Gohan disappears with Baba.

Goku does not chase them.

He cries beside the grave—not with the catastrophic grief of losing someone in battle, but with the delayed grief of the child who never understood what happened. No enemy caused this. No transformation can resolve it. No wish needs to undo it.

The tears end when they end.

Goku remains.

---

## VIII. Not Today

When Goku returns to Beerus's world, Vegeta is waiting.

“Well, Kakarot? Did you find your unbeatable opponent?”

Goku thinks before answering.

“There wasn't one.”

Vegeta smirks. “Then fight me.”

Goku smiles.

“Not today.”

Vegeta's expression changes. He understands immediately.

For the first time, Goku has declined a fight without injury, duty, hunger, or a stronger opponent pulling him elsewhere. He has not become less Saiyan. He has exercised a freedom strength could never provide.

Whis tosses an apple toward Goku without warning.

Goku's hand moves automatically and catches it.

Whis shakes his head.

“Still reacting.”

He tosses another.

Goku watches it pass.

The apple lands in Vegeta's hand.

Whis smiles.

Ultra Instinct is not reacting to everything. It is action unobstructed by ego—including the choice that no action is required.

No aura appears. Goku's hair does not change. There is no transformation.

He is simply present.

---

## IX. The Story After Goku

Years later, Pan asks Goku to train her.

She has inherited his appetite for challenge. She wants to know every transformation, defeat every rival, and follow the route everyone associates with the Son family name. Goku recognizes the motion immediately because it is his own.

He agrees to train her, but first he brings her to Mount Paozu.

They sit beside Grandpa Gohan's grave.

Pan becomes impatient. She asks when the training begins.

Goku tells her it already has.

She does not understand. She may spend an entire new series refusing to understand. Her friends—Marron, Bulla, Goten, Trunks, and Uub—will each show her a different relationship with strength. They will teach her that inheritance is not instruction, that agency matters more than transformation, and that honouring Goku does not require repeating his path.

But Goku does not force the lesson into her.

He knows now that another person's life is not an opponent to overpower with an answer.

He sits beside Pan while she fidgets.

A leaf falls between them.

Goku's body knows ten thousand ways to catch it.

He lets it land on the grave.

Pan watches.

For one quiet second, so does she.

---

## Final Image

Dragon Ball begins with a child carrying the four-star ball away from the mountain because he cannot yet understand that Grandpa Gohan is gone.

It ends with that child, now an old master, returning to the mountain and placing four stars on a grave.

Goku does not defeat death. He honours a life.

He does not break the wall. He stops treating resistance as an invitation.

He does not surpass himself. He recognizes himself.

The final transformation is transformation-less.

The final opponent is the belief that there must always be another opponent.

The final victory is Goku choosing not to fight.

And the final lesson Grandpa Gohan gives him is the first one Goku was ever given:

**You were already Goku before you became strong.**
