Recognizing Barriers and Systemic Harm

A vocabulary for identifying the progression from dehumanizing outcomes back through institutional mechanisms—and returning authority to lived experience.

Thomas Harrison / Honestly Thomas Self-Advocacy 101 · 2026
Dehumanization
Treating a person as a problem to manage instead of a human being with dignity.
Systemic Neglect
Repeated harm produced by the combined failures of policies, institutions, and fragmented responsibility.
Institutional Betrayal
Being harmed by an organization that was expected to provide safety or support.
Abandonment
Withdrawing involvement while significant needs remain unresolved.
Neglect
Failing to provide necessary care, protection, or follow-through.
Retraumatization
Recreating the fear, helplessness, or loss of control caused by earlier trauma.
Administrative Burden
Forcing someone to perform excessive paperwork, coordination, and follow-up to obtain help.
Scapegoating
Making one person carry the blame for a larger systemic failure.
Blame-Shifting
Transferring responsibility from the person or institution causing harm onto the person experiencing it.
Doublespeak
Using reassuring language to disguise harmful actions or absent support.
Gaslighting
Repeatedly manipulating someone into doubting their own memory, perception, or reality.
Gatekeeping
Controlling access to support through unnecessary barriers or subjective judgments.
Discrimination
Unequal treatment based on identity, disability, or another protected characteristic.
Ableism
Treating disabled people as less capable, credible, or valuable.
Stigma
Reducing someone to negative assumptions about their identity or diagnosis.
Tone Policing
Focusing on someone’s emotional delivery instead of what they are reporting.
Minimization
Presenting serious harm as less important than it is.
Invalidation
Denying or rejecting the reality of someone’s experience.
Dismissal
Treating someone’s concern as unworthy of serious attention.
Lived Experience
Knowledge gained by directly living through something.

Recognizing systemic harm is not pessimism. It is how lived experience becomes evidence instead of something institutions can quietly explain away.
